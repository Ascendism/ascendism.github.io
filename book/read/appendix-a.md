# Appendix A — The Window of Ascent Tool Filter

This appendix is not a purity test. It is a **decision stack** you run before a tool earns a recurring place in your week—whether that tool is a grazing app, a hosted model, a password manager, or a diesel pump you are about to finance.

The filter folds the book's recurring questions—what you are building, who benefits when a product drifts, who owns the interrupt—into one pass you can run in ten minutes at a kitchen table or in a terminal window.

**How to use it:** pick one tool under consideration. Answer each section honestly. A string of "no" answers is not a moral victory—it is a signal to redesign the task, choose a different vendor tier, or keep the offline path you already have.

---

## 1. Target alignment

- Can you state—in one sentence—which arrangement this tool serves: autonomy, land access, resilience, paid work, or access to public life?
- If the tool disappeared tomorrow, would you lose a **skill** you still need on a bad Tuesday, or only a convenience you can replace?
- Does this tool move a number you track (see Appendix C), or only a feeling?
- Would you still choose this tool if you could not post about choosing it?

---

## 2. Scale and maintenance (Schumacher-style)

E.F. Schumacher's *Small Is Beautiful* argument is often reduced to "small good, big bad." The useful slice is sharper: **right-sized for the load**, maintainable by the people who depend on it, repairable without a global supply chain miracle.

- Is the tool sized for **your** operation—not a demo farm, not a Fortune 500 procurement deck?
- Can you explain how it works to a neighbor—or to your future self at 11 p.m.—without reading marketing copy?
- Who maintains it when it breaks: you, a local tech, a vendor ticket queue, or nobody?
- If maintenance requires a subscription, is the subscription price stable relative to your cash months (Appendix C metric)?
- For physical tools: can parts be sourced regionally? For software: can you export your data in a format you can read?

---

## 3. Ownership and exit

- Do you **own** the object, the data, and the workflow—or rent all three?
- What happens at lease renewal, terms-of-service update, or price doubling? Have you read the clause that governs that moment?
- Is there an offline or self-hosted path for work that must survive a vendor email?
- If export exists, have you tested it once—not "planned to test"?
- For land-linked tools: does the lease allow the improvement you are about to finance?

---

## 4. Incentive trace

- What metric likely governs this product's roadmap: engagement, seat count, upsell, data harvest, or task completion?
- Did the tool arrive with a legitimate purpose—access, communication, productivity—and drift toward spectacle? If yes, is the original use case still supported on your tier?
- For assistive or adaptive tools: if the vendor pivots, do you have a fallback that preserves participation—not only convenience?

---

## 5. Attention and security

- Who can interrupt you through this tool—and can you silence it without a fight?
- Does the default UI train **return visits** or **task completion**?
- Are credentials for this tool behind a password manager and multi-factor authentication on the account that resets them?
- For hosted AI: does your input leave the machine? If yes, is that acceptable for the sensitivity of the data?

---

## 6. AI-specific pass (NIST AI RMF, plain language)

The National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes an **AI Risk Management Framework** (NIST AI RMF; verify current title and URL at `https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework` before print) organized around **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**. You do not need the PDF on your lap to borrow the structure.

**Govern**
- Who in your household or enterprise approves new AI uses—and who owns the mistake?
- Is there a list of tasks where AI is **off limits** (high harm, licensed profession, safety sign-off)?

**Map**
- What is the input (PDF, email, sensor log, code repo)?
- What is the output (draft, label, summary, code patch)?
- Who is downstream if the output is wrong?

**Measure**
- Have you logged one week on **one task**: minutes before, minutes after, errors caught, errors that would have shipped?
- If the log shows no gain, have you adjusted the design instead of buying a more expensive model?

**Manage**
- Where is the human verification step—and is it proportional to harm?
- Where is the archive of what shipped (email sent, code merged, rate applied to field)?

**High harm + weak verification = stop.** Medium harm + strong verification = proceed with logging. Low harm + weak verification = acceptable for drafts; still archive.

---

## 8. What this filter refuses to do

- It does not tell you to avoid all hosted tools. It tells you to **know what you rented**.
- It does not replace professional advice for law, tax, medicine, engineering, or food safety.
- It does not guarantee purity. It guarantees you looked at the blueprint before blaming the hammer.

Run the filter when you add a tool. Run it again when the vendor sends a pricing email. The window stays open only while you still know what you are building.
