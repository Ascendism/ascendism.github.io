# Chapter 5 — Data Centers, Farms, Factories, and Better Blueprints

The fight on the county line is easy to photograph. Green fields on one side of the argument. A slab of concrete and cooling towers on the other. Neighbors show up at hearings with signs about saving farmland or saving jobs, and the local paper runs a picture that makes the conflict look simple.

The simplicity is the trap.

Most of these battles are not really about whether bytes belong near corn. They are about who gets water, who gets power, who signs the lease, and who keeps the surplus when the project leaves the planning commission. The server farm and the industrial corn block are different shapes. They can still answer to the same parent: land treated as a financial asset, infrastructure sized for extraction at scale, and a public conversation trained to pick mascots instead of tracing ownership.

If you have been following the argument this far, you already know the move. Stop asking whether the thing should exist. Ask how it was designed, who benefits when it succeeds, and what happens to everyone else when the default terms hold. This chapter is where that move meets dirt, pipe, and megawatts.

---

Electricity is the honest starting point because it is measurable in a way moral panic is not. The International Energy Agency tracks the energy–AI nexus explicitly, and its reporting on data-center electricity use belongs in the primary text, not in headlines that round megawatt-hours into vibes. Data centers are a growing share of global electricity demand. The exact terawatt-hour figures move year to year as methodology tightens and operators report more honestly.

Comparative framing helps a reader without an engineering degree. The IEA publishes charts that translate data-center consumption into household-electricity equivalents so you can see scale without mistaking scale for apocalypse. A large footprint is a large footprint. It is also one load among many on a grid that already carries aluminum smelters, irrigation pumps, and winter heating peaks. The design question is not "do servers use power?" They do. The question is who pays for the upgrade, who gets the tax abatement, and whether the project returns anything durable to the watershed that supplies the cooling water.

Federal tax law is the part of that ledger hearings almost never read aloud. The rack is visible. The interconnection agreement is not. Neither is the stack of depreciation elections, production credits on a co-located solar field, and state sales-tax abatements that can move a hyperscale project from "someday" to "breaking ground this quarter."

Congress does not write a unique credit for "data center." Congressional Research Service reporting is explicit: owners may benefit from credits aimed at clean electricity, storage, efficient buildings, and manufacturing supply chains — often indirectly, through cheaper power contracts, on-site batteries, or cost-segregated equipment. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), enacted July 4, 2025, reshaped that landscape on top of the Inflation Reduction Act credits most headlines still discuss.

Three clocks matter. Conflating them is how public debate loses.

The first clock is equipment. OBBBA restored 100 percent bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025 and placed in service on or after January 20, 2025 — HVAC, electrical distribution, servers, and other assets a cost-segregation study can isolate. For federal purposes this is treated as a durable expensing rule, not a countdown to 2032. It front-loads deductions when a build's capital stack runs into the billions. Family-office and tax-equity capital pays attention even when the hyperscaler's own balance sheet does not need the shelter. That is one reason trade press after the bill described data centers as a bonus-depreciation play, not only an AI play.

The second clock is co-located wind and solar. Under pre-OBBBA IRA law, clean electricity investment and production credits (Sections 48E and 45Y) could run at full value for projects beginning construction through an "applicable year" — often summarized as the later of 2032 or an economy-wide emissions threshold, then phasing down. OBBBA accelerated wind and solar. IRS Notice 2025-42, implementing OBBBA §§70512 and 70513, requires applicable wind and solar facilities to begin construction before July 5, 2026 and generally be placed in service by December 31, 2027 or lose those credits. The data center does not claim the credit on the rack. The solar row or wind port next door does. Hyperscalers still race to pair compute with generation because a thirty-percent capital subsidy on a hundred-megawatt field changes the power price they can defend to investors.

The third clock is everything else in the power stack — nuclear power purchase agreements, geothermal, storage, efficient-building deductions — each with its own begin-construction and placed-in-service lines under OBBBA and prior law. CRS notes that other zero-emissions facilities may need construction begun before 2033 for full credits under the revised statute, with foreign-supply restrictions tightening from 2026. None of that replaces the wind-and-solar sprint; it widens the menu for counties bidding a "green" campus.

Scale is not hypothetical. U.S. data centers consumed 139 terawatt-hours in 2024, roughly forty-five percent of world data-center electricity use in CRS R48583's summary of IEA figures. Per-capita U.S. use near 540 kilowatt-hours in 2024 is projected toward 1,200 kilowatt-hours by 2030 — enough to show why every percentage point on a tax return matters at portfolio scale. Industry reporting in 2025–2026 put private data-center construction funding above thirty billion dollars in a single year, North American absorption above 2,400 megawatts, and monthly U.S. construction starts in the tens of billions. Those records track with accelerated procurement and stacked incentives, not with mascot panic alone.

Rural opportunity-zone rules under OBBBA add a geographic filter: higher basis step-ups for qualifying rural holds and new zone maps opening for investment in 2027 overlap counties where hyperscalers already hunt for grid headroom. State abatements — sales tax on servers, property-tax holidays, negotiated utility rates — stack on top. The institutional investor still writes the largest checks and may care less about bonus depreciation than a regional fund does. That does not make the federal layer decorative. It shifts who can finance the next tranche and where the term sheet lands before the public hearing.

Tax law is not the only federal lever moving hyperscale from drawing board to dirt. Executive Order 14318, signed July 23, 2025 (90 FR 35385), is the permitting half of the same sprint — a fast-track order aimed at AI and data-center infrastructure by cutting federal review delays. In plain terms, it tells agencies to treat very large AI compute buildouts as strategic infrastructure, not as a routine industrial annex that waits in line behind every other megaproject.

The order defines a Data Center Project as one that adds more than one hundred megawatts of new electrical load for AI training, inference, simulation, or synthetic data generation. That threshold alone tells you the audience. This is hyperscale territory — not a regional colocation suite or a corporate server room on an office-park slab.

The mechanics run wider than the rack. Agencies are directed to streamline environmental review, including identifying or creating National Environmental Policy Act categorical exclusions so qualifying AI infrastructure moves faster through federal review. That is one reason people worried about local control, environmental oversight, or utility strain have been talking about the order. The administration also states that federally owned land and resources may be used for rapid data-center development. And the order ties AI expansion directly to transmission lines, substations, gas infrastructure, nuclear and geothermal generation, backup power systems, semiconductors, and networking hardware. This is not only "build more servers." It is industrial policy around compute and energy together.

Projects can receive special treatment as Qualifying Projects when they involve five hundred million dollars or more in capital investment, add more than one hundred megawatts of load, protect national security, or receive a strategic designation from the Secretary of Defense, Interior, Commerce, or Energy. Possible federal financial support — loans, guarantees, grants, tax incentives — sits on that same menu. Those lines land in the same county hearing as OBBBA's depreciation clocks. A developer racing the 2026 wind-and-solar window is also racing federal permitting exemptions.

The order revoked Executive Order 14141 (January 14, 2025), a prior Biden AI infrastructure executive order, and replaced it with an explicitly deregulatory frame. Supporters call that necessary to compete with China and escape review bottlenecks. Critics call it federal acceleration of energy-hungry campuses with less local and environmental leverage. That is not abstract policy debate. It shows up in the fights you already see: grid strain, rising utility bills, water draw in dry counties, AI buildouts in rural communities where leverage was thin to begin with, transmission lines contested block by block, and the sense that a campus can appear on the calendar faster than neighbors can read the interconnection filing. EO 14318 is one mechanism feeding that perception — stacked on top of OBBBA's tax clocks, not separate from them.

When a neighbor says the build is "only about AI," ask which deadline they mean — depreciation, wind-and-solar begin-construction, or federal permitting fast-track. When a developer says the campus is "green," ask whether the renewable asset meets the 2026/2027 wind-and-solar window or a different line on the statute. When someone says the project "appeared overnight," ask whether it crossed the hundred-megawatt and five-hundred-million-dollar thresholds that trigger Qualifying Project treatment. The tool is neutral in the narrow sense. The incentive stack is not.

---

Water is where the photography stops being enough. Data centers consume water directly for cooling and indirectly through electricity generation. Operators report unevenly. Peer-reviewed and investigative work in outlets such as *The Conversation* has documented that companies rarely disclose full water use in ways the public can audit, and that water-use effectiveness (WUE) metrics — gallons per kilowatt-hour cooled — can obscure total basin draw when a facility runs flat-out in August. Regional science centers working on Nevada siting and similar dry-basin fights are useful for grounding local debates in hydrology rather than in slogan form.

If you live in a dry county, the argument you need is not "AI bad." It is a ledger: gallons per year, source water rights, peak versus average draw, and what happens to the aquifer when the next drought year arrives before the bond payments on the facility do. Someone chose this parcel, this interconnection agreement, and this public narrative. Your job is to read the lease stack, not the press release.

---

The farmland version of the same fight has its own mascot: the family farm threatened by Silicon Valley. Sometimes that story is true in the particulars. Often it is incomplete in the way that matters for design.

U.S. tenure data from USDA primary releases — not social posts — describes cropland where a large share is rented, many landlords are not farmers, and age structure points toward transfers that will reshape who decides what happens on the ground. The March 12, 2026 National Agricultural Statistics Service release from the 2024 Tenure, Ownership, and Transition of Agricultural Land (TOTAL) survey reported 348 million acres of rented farmland, with 79 percent owned by non-farming landlords who averaged 69.2 years old — more than a decade older than operator landlords at 58.1 years. Roughly 52 percent of those non-farming principal landlords had never farmed. The same survey cycle points to ownership churn: about 5 percent of the nation's roughly 900 million farmland acres — on the order of 43 million acres — expected to change hands within five years, with roughly 23 million acres expected to sell to non-relatives. ERS topic pages on farmland ownership give the structural frame across years.

None of that proves every data-center opponent is defending diversified agroecology. It proves the "save the fields" banner can hang over acreage that was already financialized before the first soil test for the server pad. In Corn Belt counties, public cropland data still shows most rented row-crop acreage in a two-crop commodity rotation — yield and hedge logic, not the diversified stewardship the green-field photograph implies. Industrial row-crop blocks and hyperscale footprints are not moral opposites. They can be two leases signed by people who will never live on the land, optimized for different spreadsheets — yield per acre here, megawatts there — with the same downstream community asked to accept externalized water and road costs.

When institutional investors accumulate farmland — a category that includes ultra-high-net-worth buyers named in popular press and anonymous LLC stacks alike — the concern for this book is structural, not personal. Land becomes an inflation hedge and a low-volatility asset. Tenant farmers meet professional asset managers. Crop mix tilts toward commodities that fit portfolio logic. Decision-making concentrates away from the people who drink the well water. If you name a famous holder, cite ownership reporting such as the Land Report and state deed research, not a chain of blog posts repeating the same acreage. If you skip names, the argument still stands: the fight over a server campus can distract from the slower enclosure happening through rent, renewal options, and easements on land that never hosted a rack.

Widen the lens. Ask who owns the parcel, who holds the water right, what crop diversity the lease requires, and where the tax revenue goes after the ribbon-cutting. A hearing that only asks "farm or factory" has already accepted a false binary.

---

Factories belong in the same chapter because the better blueprints are older than the current AI boom. Industrial symbiosis — one plant's waste becoming another's input — is taught with the Kalundborg case in Denmark for a reason. Steam, gypsum, cooling water, and fuel sludge move between partners because contracts and geography made it cheaper than throwing everything away. Nobody needed a viral hashtag to discover that heat is a resource. They needed aligned incentives, long time horizons, and engineers willing to run pipe between neighbors.

Data-center waste heat is the modern test of whether we mean any of that or only mean it when the mascot is picturesque farmland. Nordic municipalities have been more willing to run the experiment in public. Espoo, Finland, has reported district-heating projects tied to major data-center waste heat with city-level claims on the order of a large fraction of municipal heating needs — read the municipal and utility releases, not only trade press. Mäntsälä and similar Finnish cases documented by Sitra show smaller towns coupling data-center output to district networks. Denmark and Norway have integrated utility partnerships in industry reporting; triangulate against the operator and the grid company before you quote a percentage in print.

The United States has domestic examples too, but the evidence grade is thinner. Campus-scale heat recovery from multi-tenant facilities — Amazon's Seattle footprint shows up often in facilities journalism — and university supercomputing centers feeding district loops (Buffalo's Empire AI coverage, for example) are worth citing as design existence proofs, not as peer-reviewed lifecycle assessments. If you need a U.S. technical anchor at publication, search Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory data-center efficiency and water publications and prefer those over a press cycle.

What these cases share is not virtue branding. It is coupling: a second user for heat or water that would otherwise be dumped, contracts long enough to amortize pipe, and public oversight that asks what the community gets besides transient construction payroll. A data center that only extracts tax breaks and groundwater is one design. A data center built into a district-energy loop is another. The chip count is not the difference. The blueprint is.

---

Right-sizing belongs in the same sentence as reuse. Hyperscale builds dominate headlines because they dominate capex. Most ordinary work does not require the newest frontier cluster in a desert basin. Local inference, smaller models, batch jobs scheduled off-peak, and hardware kept longer than the marketing cycle suggests are not Luddism. They are load-shaping — the same discipline farmers use when they irrigate at night or run dryers when power is cheap.

Lifecycle thinking matters here. A full life-cycle assessment compares embodied energy in construction, water, grid mix, and useful work delivered. When you argue against a facility, argue with boundaries stated. When you argue for one, do the same. "Green" without boundaries is PR. "Evil" without boundaries is panic. Both let the incentive layer off the hook.

---

Low-grade waste heat can, in principle, support controlled-environment agriculture — greenhouses, aquaculture, recirculating systems — where temperature bands match species requirements. Engineering literature on waste heat to aquaculture and greenhouse loads exists in pockets; it is not as thick as district-heating case studies. Spirulina, fish, and vegetable production each carry temperature and biosecurity constraints that extension publications handle better than startup blogs.

Treat any coupling of data-center exhaust to on-site food production as a design proposal until a licensed mechanical engineer signs the heat exchanger, the food-safety plan, and the water discharge permits. The point of naming it here is not to sell a gadget. It is to show that the creative work runs toward integration — food, heat, water, and compute on one ledger — while the default sprawl model runs toward single-use extraction. You do not need to wait for perfect technology to ask your county for the integrated blueprint instead of the slab-and-exhaust blueprint.

---

You can oppose a bad siting and still refuse the mascot war. You can want farmland to stay in stewardship without pretending every acre under a "save the farm" sign was already diverse, local, or community-owned. You can want compute without gifting groundwater to a lease written in another state.

Incentives still sit below tools. Moral panic aims at the visible rack. Design choices about ownership, scale, coupling, and disclosure decide who pays when the basin drops or the tariff spikes.

Concrete questions for your next local fight or your next infrastructure decision: who owns the land and the water right, and what happens at lease renewal; what fraction of tax revenue is bonded to the project versus available for watershed maintenance; which federal clocks apply — equipment depreciation, co-located generation credits, state abatements — and what begin-construction and placed-in-service dates appear on the filed schedule; whether the project crosses EO 14318's hundred-megawatt and five-hundred-million-dollar thresholds and which NEPA or federal-land fast-tracks were invoked; whether waste heat couples to a second user or dumps; and whether the crop or industrial mix on surrounding acreage matches the stewardship story told in the hearing.

The next chapter steps into the psychological and political economy of transitional improvement — why waiting for purity freezes people who still need to eat and pay rent, and why "better for now" is not the same as selling out. The blueprints above are not excuses to stop criticizing harm. They are proof that another design is already on the map if you demand it when the ink is still wet.
